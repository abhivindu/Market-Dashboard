Government-guaranteed · liquid · rate-sensitive

Agency RMBS

▸
How it works

Pass-through securities backed by residential mortgages that carry a government or GSE guarantee (Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) against default risk - so unlike every other asset class in this report, credit risk is essentially not the story here. The entire investment case is interest-rate and prepayment risk: when rates fall, homeowners refinance and return principal early (reinvestment risk at lower yields); when rates rise, prepayments slow and duration extends just as rates are moving against you (negative convexity, the defining technical feature of this asset class).

Market size & issuance

MBS issuance reached $337.7B year-to-date through February 2026, up 16.2% y/y; agency MBS trading volume averaged $396.8B daily, up 7.2% y/y - both signal healthy market liquidity and demand.

Performance & spread commentary

Mixed across 2026 so far: the Bloomberg U.S. MBS Index returned +0.07% in April (+0.24% excess return to Treasuries) but -1.42% in July, when sector option-adjusted spread (OAS) widened 7bp to +31bp - a real, sourced spread move, not a proxy. Agency MBS is coming off its best year since 2002 (+8.5% in 2025), and PIMCO's lead portfolio manager describes 2026 valuations as still 'cheap on average' even after that rally.

Institutional commentary

PIMCO (Dan Hyman, managing director): 'We continue to see good opportunities in the agency mortgage in 2026, with valuations on average looking cheap and differentiation of bonds within the market creating good relative value opportunities for active managers' (2026). Separately, PIMCO frames agency MBS as 'an important defensive anchor in the portfolio' given tight corporate spreads elsewhere - a relative-value argument, not an absolute one.

Institutional investment activity

PIMCO's Total Return Fund and Income Fund both hold ~18% in agency MBS as of June 30, 2026. PIMCO's actively-managed MBS ETF (PMBS, $1.3B AUM) drew over $800M in inflows over the trailing year - a clear signal of institutional/advisor rotation into active MBS management specifically, not just passive exposure.

Fed-decision sensitivity

High and structurally amplified by negative convexity. Agency MBS effective duration runs roughly 5-6 years currently, so a 25bp Fed-driven rate move implies a rough 1.25-1.5% price impact before convexity - but because prepayments slow when rates rise, duration extends further right when you don't want it to, meaning realized losses in a selloff tend to exceed the simple duration math, while gains in a rally are capped by refinancing. The Fed's own balance sheet actions matter directly here too: the Fed cut its Reserve Management Purchases to zero starting August 14, 2026, removing a buyer from the market at the margin.

Headwinds & tailwinds

Tailwind: the Trump administration's GSE MBS purchase program (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac authorized to buy $200B of agency MBS, announced January 8, 2026) tightened spreads and boosted 2026 performance, though PIMCO notes conviction has 'moderated a bit' now that spreads are narrower post-rally. Headwind: continued Fed MBS runoff (holdings fell below $2 trillion by end of Q1 2026) keeps a structural seller/non-buyer in the market with banks 'comparatively inactive,' which is why spreads remain unusually wide by historical standards even with the GSE program's support.

Sentiment & outlook

Constructive but not uncomplicated - PIMCO's own framing captures the tension well: cheap on an absolute/relative basis and a genuine defensive anchor, but the spread-widening episode in July 2026 shows the asset class is still exposed to real volatility when technicals (Fed runoff, bank demand) turn unfavorable even without any credit-quality story at all.

Non-Agency RMBS

▸
How it works

Residential mortgage pools without a government/GSE guarantee - real credit risk, unlike agency RMBS. Deals pool 500-2,000 mortgage loans into senior-subordinate structures, where subordination (junior tranches absorb losses first) and performance triggers insulate senior bondholders from collateral volatility. The largest growth segment is non-QM (non-Qualified Mortgage) - loans to borrowers who don't fit conventional agency underwriting boxes (self-employed, investor property, bank-statement income) but aren't necessarily lower credit quality.

Market size & issuance

Non-agency RMBS volume hit $192B through September 11, 2026, with $83B of that in non-QM - already a record for the category, beating all of 2025's $80B in just over 8 months. Non-QM has grown from a niche post-crisis product into a ~$200B market, with ~$100B of issuance expected this year, equivalent to roughly 7% of GSE gross issuance.

Performance & spread commentary

Spreads have tightened materially: non-QM AAA tranches tightened to 123bp and AA to 140bp as of mid-September 2026 - real, sourced spread levels, not a proxy. Credit performance has also improved sharply year over year: 2026 YTD delinquency rates of 0.37% (prime) and 1.62% (non-QM) compare favorably to the stressed 2023 vintage, which saw 3.01% (prime) and 10.29% (non-QM) delinquencies surpass pre-pandemic highs.

Institutional commentary

Guggenheim Investments (Q3 2026 Structured Credit Outlook): non-agency RMBS borrowers 'skew toward the higher income cohort of the population with better credit scores' and are 'less sensitive to the stresses emerging in consumer credit' among lower-income borrowers - a favorable relative-credit-quality argument. Guggenheim's institutional positioning explicitly favors 'senior non-Agency RMBS securities' for income generation given 'favorable credit fundamentals, reduced interest-rate sensitivity, and appealing valuations.'

Institutional investment activity

Guggenheim's Securitized Income ETF (GISC) and broader securitized-credit strategies carry active non-agency RMBS positioning per its Q2/Q3 2026 sector views. Angel Oak Mortgage Solutions - the leading non-QM originator - is targeting 50% year-over-year origination growth in 2026, funding continued record securitization volume (its Angel Oak Mortgage Trust program priced deals like AOMT 2026-3, a $279.6M non-prime RMBS, and AOMT 2026-5 at $228.2M in 2026 alone).

Fed-decision sensitivity

Lower direct rate sensitivity than agency RMBS - Guggenheim specifically cites 'reduced interest-rate sensitivity' as part of the sector's appeal, partly because non-QM borrowers face 'structural disincentives to prepay' (many carry prepayment penalties or non-conforming rates that don't benefit as much from refinancing waves). The bigger Fed-linked risk runs through credit, not duration: if Fed policy tightens financial conditions enough to push unemployment higher, that's the historically dominant driver of RMBS delinquency spikes (the 2023 vintage stress was linked to 'an upward shift in the jobless rate'), not rate moves directly.

Headwinds & tailwinds

Tailwind: record investor demand has tightened non-QM spreads meaningfully while credit performance has actually improved year over year - an unusually clean combination of rising volume, tightening spreads, and better fundamentals simultaneously. Headwind: 'fragmented non-QM origination and elevated 2026 supply could strain syndication capacity' - the same record issuance that signals strength could eventually outrun dealer/investor capacity to absorb it if the pace continues.

Sentiment & outlook

Constructive and credit-differentiated from consumer credit broadly - the Guggenheim thesis (better-credit-quality borrowers, less exposed to the stress hitting lower-income consumers elsewhere) is a specific, falsifiable claim worth checking against future delinquency prints rather than a generic bullish view, and the current 0.37%/1.62% delinquency data supports it for now.

Commercial real estate · tightening spreads, rising distress

CMBS Conduit / Multi-Borrower

▸
How it works

Multiple commercial mortgage loans (office, retail, multifamily, hotel, industrial - diversified property types and sponsors) pooled into one securitization, structured into senior-subordinate tranches by credit rating. Diversification across many loans/borrowers is the core structural difference from single-asset/single-borrower (SASB) deals - a conduit deal's senior tranches are insulated as long as losses stay concentrated in a minority of the underlying loans.

Market size & issuance

YTD 2026 private-label CMBS and CRE CLO issuance totaled $103.9B, up 21% from $85.7B in the same period last year; total CMBS issuance (conduit + SASB + CRE CLO) is on track to exceed $70B by mid-year and Q1 2026 alone came in at ~$22B, +25% y/y - a clear rebound narrative on the volume side.

Performance & spread commentary

A genuine tension worth naming directly: spreads have tightened sharply even as delinquencies hit a post-COVID high. AAA conduit spreads compressed to T+100-115bp in Q1 2026 (from T+125-140bp in late 2025), with some reporting citing spreads as tight as T+70bp more recently - real, sourced spread levels. At the same time, the overall CMBS delinquency rate reached 7.55% in May 2026, the highest since the pandemic, and the distress rate (delinquencies + specially-serviced, non-current loans) hit 12.07% in March 2026, a new cycle high. Office is the clear outlier: delinquencies there hit an all-time high of 12.34% in January before dipping only slightly to 11.4% in February.

Institutional commentary

CRE Finance Council (CREFC), the industry's primary trade association, publishes monthly loan-performance updates (cited throughout this section) that are the closest thing to an institutional consensus read on conduit CMBS health. Their data underpins the widely-cited finding that debt yields - not missed payments - are the dominant risk signal: roughly 36% of 2026's hard maturities (~$27.3B) carry debt yields of 8% or below, the threshold research identifies as the highest-risk refinancing zone, meaning many distressed loans are still current on payments even as they face near-certain maturity default.

Institutional investment activity

Blackstone's Real Estate Debt Strategies V (BREDS V) closed at $8B in March 2026 - matching Blackstone's 2020 record - with CalPERS committing $1.5B and Illinois, New Mexico, and Ohio public pension funds also participating. The fund is explicitly targeting the $1.5 trillion wave of CRE debt maturities, providing bridge/mezzanine financing and buying distressed loan portfolios from banks/insurers reducing CRE exposure - institutional capital positioning to be the liquidity provider for exactly the maturity-wall stress described above.

Fed-decision sensitivity

High and two-directional: CMBS loan coupons at origination and floating-rate CMBS/CRE-CLO tranches both move directly with Fed policy, while fixed-rate conduit bond prices carry meaningful duration risk (typical conduit deal duration runs roughly 4-7 years depending on tranche). The more acute Fed-linked risk in 2026 specifically is refinancing math, not duration: with ~39% of this year's $76.6-100B+ hard maturities concentrated in Q4 2026, any Fed move that keeps refinancing rates elevated directly worsens the ~$57.7B in loans already estimated likely to default at maturity simply because sponsors can't refinance cash-flowing properties at current rates against depressed appraised values (office values down 55.8% since issuance in some appraisals).

Headwinds & tailwinds

Tailwind: institutional capital (Blackstone's $8B BREDS V, tightening AAA spreads, rebounding issuance) is clearly underwriting a recovery thesis and stepping in as a liquidity provider ahead of the maturity wall. Headwind: the maturity wall itself is real and imminent - over $100B in CMBS loans mature in 2026, more than half by dollar volume ($57.7B) estimated likely to default at maturity given depressed office valuations, and nearly 39% of that maturity volume lands in Q4 2026, concentrating the risk window rather than spreading it out.

Sentiment & outlook

Bifurcated by property type more than by the market as a whole - broad issuance/spread technicals look genuinely healthy (rebounding volume, tightening AAA spreads, institutional capital raising at record pace), but that headline strength is coexisting with, not resolving, acute office-sector distress. Multifamily delinquencies actually improved in February even as they later ticked up 30bp in March per Trepp, underscoring how property-type-specific this cycle is rather than a uniform CMBS story.

CMBS Single-Asset/Single-Borrower (SASB)

▸
How it works

A securitization backed by a single large property or portfolio under one borrower, rather than a diversified pool - investors are making a concentrated bet on one asset's cash flows and value rather than a diversified basket. This concentration is priced: SASB tranches typically require deeper credit enhancement than conduit for the same rating, but in exchange investors get full transparency into one asset rather than exposure diluted across many.

Market size & issuance

SASB has become the dominant CMBS format: it accounted for more than 75% of the $76.2B in total private-label CMBS issuance through the first seven months of 2026. Within SASB, data centers are the fastest-growing property type - $4.9B in H1 2026 issuance, ~9% of all SASB volume and ~7.5% of overall CMBS issuance, up from a $4B base for the entire data-center ABS+CMBS category as recently as 2020.

Performance & spread commentary

Real, sourced spread compression: some prime SASB deals have priced senior tranches sub-T+100bp, tighter than benchmark conduit AAA spreads of T+125-150bp over the same period - SASB is pricing through conduit for the best assets, a genuine quality-premium signal. Within SASB itself, office is recovering selectively: 14 New York single-asset office loans accounted for 60% of $18.3B in private-label office CMBS issued through early August 2026, with SASB office debt ($15.6B, 85.4% of that total) carrying a median 9.5% debt yield at origination - well below the 15.6% median debt yield for conduit office loans, suggesting SASB lenders are underwriting materially higher-quality (lower-risk) office collateral than the conduit market overall.

Institutional commentary

PGIM ('The Shifting CMBS Landscape: SASB at the Helm'): frames SASB's rise to CMBS-format dominance as structural, not cyclical - large, well-capitalized borrowers increasingly prefer the transparency and potentially tighter execution of a single-asset deal over pooling into a conduit. Trepp's Stephen Buschbom and Andy Boettcher (September 2026 interview) characterize 2026 as a 'busy year for CMBS' with office deals leading growth through July before data centers 'arrived' as a distinct, fast-scaling category.

Institutional investment activity

Morgan Stanley projects ~$28B in US data-center net issuance in 2026 (plus $5.5B refinancing, ~$33.5B gross); JPMorgan projects the category could reach $30-40B annually in both 2026 and 2027. The financing need behind this is enormous: hyperscalers (Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, AWS) are investing $700B in 2026 alone on cloud/AI infrastructure, with roughly $1.5 trillion of the total buildout expected to require external financing beyond hyperscaler cash flow - SASB CMBS and data-center ABS are two of the vehicles absorbing that financing need. Barclays projects data-center securitizations could reach $180B outstanding by year-end 2028, ~15% of the entire SASB CMBS market by then.

Fed-decision sensitivity

Similar duration profile to conduit CMBS (roughly 5-10 years for large SASB deals, often longer for data-center/infrastructure assets with long hyperscaler lease terms) but the more distinctive Fed-linked risk here is cap-rate/valuation sensitivity: SASB data-center and trophy-office deals are priced on long-duration income streams (multi-year hyperscaler or anchor-tenant leases), so a sustained higher-for-longer rate environment compresses valuations more than it would for shorter-duration conduit collateral, even though the underlying tenant credit (investment-grade hyperscalers) is generally stronger than a diversified conduit pool's typical borrower base.

Headwinds & tailwinds

Tailwind: data-center SASB is riding the same AI-infrastructure capex wave covered elsewhere in this dashboard (Point 2's frontier-tech coverage, Point 3's equities desk) directly into the structured-credit market - a genuine cross-asset-class theme, not an isolated CRE story. Tailwind: SASB's underwriting quality (lower debt yields on office specifically) suggests lenders are being more selective than the conduit market broadly. Headwind: concentration risk is structural to the format - a single-tenant or single-property SASB deal has zero diversification if that hyperscaler relocates, that anchor tenant defaults, or that one asset's value is impaired, unlike a conduit pool that can absorb individual loan losses.

Sentiment & outlook

Constructive and thematically connected to the strongest secular story in this entire dashboard (AI-driven data-center demand) - but concentration risk is the real, structural tradeoff for the tighter pricing and stronger current performance SASB is showing versus conduit, worth remembering precisely because the sector's growth narrative is so compelling right now.

Auto Loan ABS

▸
How it works

Pools of auto loans (new/used vehicle purchase financing) securitized into rated tranches. The credit story here is unusually bifurcated by design: prime and subprime auto ABS are effectively two different risk universes bundled under one asset-class label, since subprime borrowers are far more exposed to income/employment shocks than prime borrowers with established credit.

Market size & issuance

US ABS market issuance (auto is the largest single sub-category) reached $358B YTD in 2025, +16.8% y/y. Over 80% of the US ABS market carries an A rating or higher, with 55% specifically AAA-rated - a highly senior-tranche-dominated market structurally, even within the more credit-stressed subprime segment.

Performance & spread commentary

Sharp bifurcation, with real sourced numbers on both sides: prime AAA 3-year notes priced at T+40bp as of March 26, 2026, with prime AAA spreads narrowing 5bp year-over-year - genuine strength at the top of the credit stack. Subprime tells a very different story: 60+ day delinquencies hit ~6.9-7.0% in early 2026, the highest level since 1994 and above the peaks seen in the 2008 financial crisis; annualized net losses reached 9.81% as of the January 2026 index, up from 9.51% a year earlier. Rates did ease somewhat to 5.5% by May 2026, but Fitch still expects auto ABS delinquencies to run higher in 2026 than 2025 overall.

Institutional commentary

Janus Henderson ('Looking under the hood of U.S. auto ABS'): notes auto ABS currently offers a spread premium of roughly 35bp over similarly-rated short-duration credit generally, and frames prime borrowers (~75% of the auto ABS market) as 'remain[ing] on solid footing' even as subprime shows recession-like stress - the same bifurcation theme independently confirmed by the delinquency data. Fitch Ratings has separately flagged how tariff-driven new-car price increases ($3,000-$10,000 per Trump-era tariffs) could paradoxically support used-vehicle collateral values (and therefore auto ABS recovery rates) even as it pressures new-vehicle affordability and origination volumes.

Institutional investment activity

Castlelake, a tenured pure-play asset-based private credit manager, closed its Asset-Based Private Credit III fund at $2.3B (exceeding its $2B target) with pension funds and sovereign wealth funds as backers, deploying across consumer/auto-adjacent asset-based lending among other strategies. Separately, Castlelake-managed funds agreed in July 2026 to purchase up to $4B in AI-underwritten consumer loans from fintech lender Upstart over 24 months - one of the largest institutional commitments to AI-underwritten consumer credit disclosed to date, and directly relevant to how subprime-adjacent consumer lending is being underwritten and funded going forward.

Fed-decision sensitivity

Moderate and asymmetric by credit tier. Auto ABS duration is short (typically 1-3 years for prime, similar or shorter for subprime given faster prepayment/default-driven runoff), so direct rate/duration sensitivity is limited versus longer-duration structured product. The more consequential Fed linkage runs through the consumer: elevated rates for longer directly strain subprime borrowers' debt-to-income ratios (the driver behind the delinquency data above), while a Fed easing cycle would likely show up first and most visibly in subprime performance metrics rather than in prime, which is already 'on solid footing' per Janus Henderson.

Headwinds & tailwinds

Tailwind: prime segment spreads are tightening and represent ~75% of the market - the majority of auto ABS collateral is performing well regardless of the subprime headlines. Tailwind (paradoxical): tariff-driven new car price increases may support used-car collateral values, cushioning subprime recovery rates even as affordability worsens elsewhere. Headwind: subprime delinquency at a 32-year high is a genuine, ongoing credit event - not a one-quarter blip - and Fitch's own 2026-over-2025 higher-delinquency forecast suggests the stress isn't expected to resolve quickly.

Sentiment & outlook

A genuinely two-tier market rather than one story - prime auto ABS is a quiet, tightening-spread, institutionally-favored segment, while subprime is living through its worst delinquency environment in over three decades. Any single 'auto ABS is fine' or 'auto ABS is stressed' headline is likely oversimplifying which tier it's actually describing.
